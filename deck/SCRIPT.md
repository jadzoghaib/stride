# The script

Nineteen presented slides in fifteen minutes. Every slide has a target and a
running clock, because the only way to find out this is too long is to say it
out loud against a timer.

The same words are in each slide's speaker notes, so Presenter View carries
them on the day. This file is for rehearsal, where you want the whole thing in
one scroll and a sense of where you should be.

Pace assumption is about 135 words a minute, which is slower than conversation
and roughly right for a defence. If you are naturally faster, you will gain
thirty seconds and should spend them on slides 7 and 9 rather than hurrying.

## 1 · Cover · 0:30

Good morning. I'm Jad Zoghaib, and this is Stride.

Two sentences for the whole thing. Get the athlete paid. Show the brand what
it bought.

The athletes are semi-professional, in niche sports, with a real following and
no agent. The brands are the businesses near their club.

Everything I show you is live at that URL, and I'm happy to open it in the
questions.

## 2 · Why this · 0:35

Why this, and it starts with one person.

A friend I play rugby with is the CrossFit champion of Lebanon, and one of the
best rugby league players in the country. **Neither sport pays him.**

I want to be precise, because the easy version of this is a big zero and it
wouldn't be true. He gets given things. Gear, product, the odd paid post. What
none of it has is a price, a contract, or any reason to arrive a second time.

He's one of the five athletes in my research, and in that group he isn't the
exception. He's the median. Someone at the very top of two sports, with a real
audience, and no mechanism anywhere that turns either of those into income.

I started building this because of him.

*[touch the bottom line, don't dwell]* And the reason I can see it at all:
these are teammates, not survey respondents. I'm capped for Lebanon myself,
which is why the conversations were candid. The consulting built the model, and
the demo is my own code.

## 3 · Given, not paid · 0:45

He was one of five athletes in my own sports I asked the same question: what
does your sport actually pay you.

They do get things. Gear, product, the occasional paid post, prize money now and
then. A national champion is given plenty.

What none of it has is a rate, a contract, or any reason at all to expect it a
second time. **A champion gets given things. Nobody is buying anything.** And
you can't build a career, or a business, on being given things.

One of the five does have a proper sponsor, and he didn't get it as a rugby
league player. It arrived some other way, and no part of it came through the
sport.

*[beat, then the quote]* And read the quote, because it's the finding that
matters most. They don't build an audience, because there's no return on
building one.

That isn't a complaint about money. It's a market with no infrastructure in it.
And look at the second finding: because they stop building, the market measures
smaller than it really is. Every number after this is the conservative reading.

## 4 · The buyer's half · 1:05

Now the other side, and this is the half of the company I care most about.

The brand paying for this isn't Nike. It's a physiotherapy clinic, a gym, a car
dealer near the athlete's club. They have a budget. What they don't have is any
instrument at all.

They can't **choose**: two athletes, and nothing to compare them on but
follower count, which is the one number you can buy. They can't **price**:
there's no rate card anywhere in the segment. And they can't **verify**: the
campaign ends and they know nothing they didn't know before it started.

I'll be straight about status. I've interviewed athletes, not sponsors. That's
the biggest hole in my research, so it's the first thing I test, and I've
written down in advance which answer kills it: if brands tell me they already
know what they get, my differentiator is gone.

## 5 · One missing layer · 0:45

So why does this happen on both sides at once? Because one layer is missing,
and it used to do two jobs.

An agent introduces the athlete to the brand, and an agent vouches for what the
brand is getting. In football that costs ten to twenty per cent and it exists.
In trail running or rugby league there's no agent at all. So the athlete has no
route to income, and the brand has no basis for a decision.

One absence. Two victims.

## 6 · Two engines · 1:00

So what is Stride. Two engines, and I want to be precise that they're peers
rather than a product and a feature.

Engine one is monetisation. The athlete gets their own page with paid fan tiers
they price themselves. They keep eighty-five per cent and pay nothing to be
there. That's forty-eight per cent of year seven revenue.

Engine two is analytics. A sponsor briefs a campaign, gets athletes matched on
measured evidence with every score decomposed, then gets delivery measured
against what was projected. Ten per cent of the deal plus a subscription, and
that's the other fifty-two.

One deliberate choice: no athlete fee, ever. The athlete earning two hundred a
month pays the same rate as the one earning five thousand. A subscription would
have been regressive against exactly the athlete we're built for.

## 7 · The flywheel · 1:15 · **the one to protect**

This is the slide I'd most like you to remember.

Follow the loop. Fans pay the athlete, so the athlete has a reason to build an
audience. To take that money they connect their platforms, and those
connectors are the same instrument that measures the audience. That measurement
is what the sponsor sees instead of a follower count. The deal raises the
athlete's income, which brings the next athlete, who arrives with another
audience to measure. And four returns to one.

Now the part that matters commercially. A creator platform has no reason to
measure an audience, so it never builds this. A sponsorship data company has no
relationship with the athlete, so it can't get the data at all. You need both
halves, and almost nobody has both.

And the financing consequence, which is the sentence to keep if you keep only
one: fan revenue funds the years in which the data accrues. By the time the
analytics are worth selling, someone else has already paid to build them.

## 8 · It is built · 0:40

This is the part most business plans can't show you.

It isn't a mockup. It's deployed, the URL is on the slide, and there's a QR
code if you want it on your phone right now. Fourteen demo accounts,
registration open.

What you're looking at is an athlete's marketability score, seventy-two, as
the mean of five computed dimensions, each one decomposed, with the connected
platforms and the live offers underneath. Every number on that screen traces
back to a platform sync.

## 9 · What the sponsor sees · 1:10 · **the proof**

And the same proof for the second engine.

This is the screen a sponsor shows their own management. Delivered reach,
engagements, cost per thousand reached, cost per engagement. Underneath, every
athlete on the campaign with what they actually delivered against what was
projected when the offer was priced.

*[point at the red figure]* I want to point at one number. Minus eighty-five
point eight per cent. That's a deal that underdelivered, and the platform is
telling the sponsor so. Every incentive in this industry runs the other way,
and that's the number that makes the rest of the screen believable.

And look at the dashes. A deal with nothing attached reads as unmeasured, never
as zero. Zero would be a lie that flatters us.

One honesty note, and I'd rather say it than have you find it: the engagement
figures in the demo are seeded, and the product labels them simulated on the
screen itself. The arithmetic is real. The data is a fixture.

## 10 · The expert · 0:35

One expert interview changed the product.

Íñigo Cristóbal Losada, AI lead at olympics.com, eleven years in Olympic
broadcasting. I pitched him a product for the IOC, Olympic athletes only. He
rejected it outright and told me to open registration to everyone but filter
who gets in.

The admission gate you just saw exists because of that sentence. The research
changed the product; it didn't decorate it.

## 11 · Market · 0:45

Market, bottom up, because top down doesn't work here.

European sport sponsorship is twenty-four point eight billion, and Spain is the
fastest-growing major market at plus fourteen per cent. But none of that breaks
below the professional tier, so I refuse to divide it by a number and call it
my market.

Instead: a hundred and thirty-eight thousand niche-sport athletes in Europe
over five thousand followers. Fifty-five thousand in our six launch markets.
Six thousand three hundred by year seven, which is eleven per cent.
Deliberately modest.

## 12 · The wedge · 0:50

Why niche first. Two reasons, and the second is the better one.

The first is cost. Sixteen euros to acquire a niche athlete against forty for a
popular one, and by year seven forty-eight per cent of them monetise against
thirty. A professional already has an agent and wouldn't use this, so nobody is
competing for them.

The second is the one I'd argue for. A niche athlete's audience is concentrated
where they train and compete. To a national advertiser that's close to
worthless, which is why nobody bids for it. To the clinic two streets from the
club it's the only thing worth buying, and there is no other way to buy it.

The same decision makes my supply cheap and my demand side exist at all.

## 13 · Competition · 0:50

I'll be straight about the comparables, because they aren't flattering.

Four companies have built here. The two doing exactly what we do are small
after more than a decade. Sponsoo has thirteen people after eleven years and
its total equity is zero.

But look at how the four split. Two built the marketplace, two built the data,
nobody built both. That's the flywheel slide again.

On SponsorUnited, the short answer: they sell what the market did, we measure
what one brand's money did, and we can only do that because the projection is
written onto the deal when the offer is made. I'm not beating them. I'm serving
a customer their cost structure won't let them near.

And none of the four has the fan.

## 14 · Revenue mix · 0:35

The revenue model in one picture. Three streams.

Fan subscriptions in amber, sponsorship commission and the sponsor SaaS tier
compounding behind them.

*[get ahead of the invisible Y1 bar]* Year one is too small to see on this
axis, and it's worth knowing why: year one is **entirely sponsorship**. Fans
start paying in year two.

The shape is the point: this starts as a sponsorship business, becomes a
subscription business, and ends as a two-sided one. Seventy-five per cent fan
in year two, forty-eight by year seven. Three million of net revenue.

## 15 · Year one · 0:40

Year one, concretely, because a plan that can't say what happens first isn't a
plan.

Two hundred and fifty athletes from twelve hundred and fifty applications, in
two or three niche Spanish sports. Twenty sponsor conversations that are the
first sales as much as the research. A hundred and thirteen thousand euros, two
thirds of it a non-dilutive ENISA loan. One salary, mine, plus an anchor athlete
on equity rather than cash.

And note the build order, because it's counter-intuitive: sponsorship rail
first, fan product last, even though the fan product is the thesis. Commission
earns inside year one. The fan tier can't.

## 16 · Operations and people · 0:30 · **first to cut**

Operations and people, briefly.

Payments, compute and media are all subcontracted. The only human step in the
whole chain is admission review, and it scales with applications rather than
transactions. That's why sixteen people can run this.

And I'll name the weak point before you do: three hundred and one thousand of
revenue per employee at year seven. If that's wrong, headcount is what gives,
and the consequence is slower growth, not insolvency.

## 17 · Financials · 0:45

The financials. Revenue in grey, EBITDA the amber line.

Loss-making for four years, and the losses are small: the worst year is a
hundred and forty-two thousand. EBITDA positive in year four, cash in year
five, and year seven is three million at a twenty-four point seven per cent
margin.

One honest note on margin. We climb to seventy per cent, not the eighty a SaaS
business would claim, because we move real money and Stripe takes its cut. I'd
rather show you seventy that survives diligence.

## 18 · The ask · 0:55

The ask. A hundred and fifty thousand at one point eight million pre-money, and
it's the first of two.

Look at the curve. The worst point is two hundred and ninety-six thousand in
year three. Total requirement with a forty per cent buffer is four hundred and
fourteen. But I don't need it now.

A hundred and fifty now, which also unlocks a seventy-five thousand ENISA loan
at no equity cost. Then two hundred and fifty in year two, priced against three
months of real subscription revenue instead of against the same promise I'm
making today. That staging costs me about two points of equity and it's worth
it.

The important property: the pre-seed clears the trough on its own. Every round
after it is a growth option, not a rescue.

## 19 · Close · 0:50

I'll finish where the plan is weakest, because you're going to ask anyway.

One assumption carries this. Will fans of a semi-professional athlete actually
pay? No interview I did answers that. No market sizing answers it. Only real
subscription revenue does.

So the plan is arranged so that question costs eighty thousand euros and one
year to answer, instead of two point six million and four. If the answer is no,
what's left is a smaller sponsorship marketplace: viable, but a different
company, and I'd rather tell you that than find out quietly.

Everything else is already built. Thank you, and I'm happy to open the demo.

**Four of the twenty-three questions with no backup slide, worth rehearsing anyway.**

- **"Fifteen deals from twenty conversations is a seventy-five per cent close
  rate."** Say it is the most aggressive number in year one, that the twenty are
  warm rather than cold because they come through the anchor athlete's club, and
  that if it is half that, year one commission is €800 instead of €1,609, which
  changes nothing structural, because year one revenue is rounding either way.
- **"You say the value is local, but your product reports country."** True, and
  say so. The locality is in which athlete the brand picks, not in the
  measurement. The country split is explicitly an estimate, derived by weighting
  the athlete's own audience mix by delivered reach, and the product labels it as
  a derivation rather than a measurement. Finer geography is not available from
  any platform at post level.
- **"Your Y7 target is 6,300 across three markets, but your SAM is six
  markets."** Agreed, the percentage is conservative for that reason. Against the
  three markets actually entered it is a larger share, and the plan reports the
  cautious version.
- **"Why can't Sponsoo just add fan subscriptions?"** They can. It would take
  them the payment rail, the moderation stack and the safeguarding work, and it
  would not give them the connector data, because their athletes have no reason
  to link accounts. The order matters: we monetise in order to measure.
